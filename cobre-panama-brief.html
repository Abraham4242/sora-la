The story in one page.

A mine that produced 1.5% of global copper, 5% of Panama's GDP, and triggered the largest protest movement in modern Panamanian history. Closed since November 2023. Now slowly, partially, returning.

THE COMPANY
First Quantum Minerals · Toronto

Canadian copper major. TSX: FM. Founded 1996 by Philip and Tristan Pascall family. Operations in Zambia (Kansanshi, Sentinel), Panama (closed), Spain (closed), Mauritania (closed). Cobre Panamá was 40% of global revenue pre-closure. Market cap ~CAD $14-20B.

THE MINE
Cobre Panamá · Donoso, Colón

Open-pit copper porphyry. 13,600 hectares. 3.1 billion tonnes of proven reserves at 0.37% Cu. Own port (Punta Rincón), 300-MW power plant, 56 km internal roads. Caribbean concentrate ships to Asia/Europe. Commercial production: Sep 2019.

THE OWNERSHIP
FQM 90% + KORES 10%

Operated by Minera Panamá S.A. (MPSA). Korea Resources Corporation has held 10% since 2017 (originally 20% with LS-Nikko Copper). Jiangxi Copper (China) owns 19.5% of FQM parent — largest shareholder.

THE STATUS
Closed · partial unlock April 2026

Closure ordered Nov 28, 2023 after Supreme Court ruled the operating contract unconstitutional. Care + maintenance: 1,600 workers. April 7, 2026: Panama formally approved processing of 38 Mt stockpile (70,000 t recoverable Cu). Full restart decision: expected June 2026.

THE CORE TENSION · 2026

Panama needs the mine economically — it was 5% of GDP and 75% of exports. First Quantum needs the mine for its global cash flow — it was 40% of revenue. But the constitutional ruling, the protest movement, and the new fiscal terms make any restart politically expensive. The path through is being negotiated this year.

ARBITRATION
FQM dropped ICC, suspended FTA

March 2025: FQM discontinued the ICC arbitration ($20B claim) and suspended the Canada-Panama FTA claim — preconditions Mulino set for talks.

STOCKPILE
70,000 t Cu unlocked

April 2026: 38 Mt of pre-shutdown ore approved for processing. ~$250M capex. 700 new direct jobs. ~1 year processing.

DECISION
June 2026 announcement

Mulino administration awaiting SGS audit (delayed past May 29). Public opinion has flipped: negative view of mine dropped from 80%+ to ~50%.

05 · ORIGINS · 1968 → 2013

From UN survey to a Canadian major.

The deposit was discovered before most of today's miners were born. Its title chain runs through a colorful Panamanian businessman, a Canadian junior, a Toronto mid-tier, and finally First Quantum.

1968
UN–Panama mineral mission discovers the deposit

A joint United Nations and Government of Panama geological survey identifies the Petaquilla porphyry copper deposit during a national mineral exploration program (1956–1982). Panama was looking for alternative revenue streams.

1987
Richard Fifer founds Adrian Resources

Panamanian geologist Richard Fifer Carles (born 1957, University of Utah) — later called the "father of modern Panamanian mining" — founds the company that will become Petaquilla Minerals.

Aug 7, 1991
Exploration contract 27-A signed under President Endara

Fifer's Geo-Recursos Internacional SA gets exploration rights for Coclé del Norte and San José del General in Donoso, Colón. Ministry of Commerce official Roberto Alfaro signs the contract.

Feb 26, 1997
Law 9 of 1997 — original 25-year extraction contract

National Assembly under President Ernesto Pérez Balladares ratifies the mining contract for Minera Petaquilla S.A. (formerly Geo-Recursos). Royalty rate set at ~$0.30/tonne — later called "scandalously low."

2005
Petaquilla splits into Gold + Copper

Petaquilla Gold begins gold mining at Cerro Petaquilla (Molejón) — without an approved environmental impact study. Petaquilla Copper continues in joint venture with Inmet Mining and Teck.

2008
Inmet consolidates the copper project

Inmet Mining Corporation (Toronto) buys out Teck's interest. Petaquilla Copper renamed Cobre Panamá under Inmet.

2010
ANAM $1.9M environmental fine — annulled

Panama's environmental authority fines Petaquilla Gold for damage near the mine — Supreme Court annuls the sanction. Prior reports documented 80% forest cover loss on 160 hectares; ANAM called environmental damage "irreversible."

2012
Korea Panama Mining JV buys 20%

Korea Resources Corp (KORES) and LS-Nikko Copper form a 50/50 JV (Korea Panama Mining) and acquire a 20% interest in the project from Inmet. Korean offtake rights secured.

2013
First Quantum acquires Inmet for C$5.1 billion

Hostile-then-friendly takeover. The deal is essentially about acquiring the Cobre Panamá project. First Quantum now holds 80% of Minera Panamá S.A. (MPSA), with KORES + LS-Nikko holding 20%.

2017
FQM acquires LS-Nikko's 10% for $635M

LS-Nikko Copper exits its equity stake in MPSA over six installments. KORES retains 10%. Final ownership: FQM 90%, KORES 10%.

FIFER'S POLITICAL ARC

Fifer rotated through every administration: under Pérez Balladares (1994-99) he secured a 60-year operating license; under Mireya Moscoso (1999-2004) he became Coclé Governor; in 2005 he had an arrest warrant for stealing $100,000 from the governor's office (resolved by returning funds); under Torrijos (2004-09) he revived mine work at Molejón; under Martinelli (2009-14) Petaquilla Gold got its export license. Later became Chairman of CODEMIN, Panama's state mining company.

06 · THE BUILD + THE CRASH · 2013 → 2023

A decade of capex, then a year of collapse.

$10 billion invested. Four years of construction. Four years of production. Then a Supreme Court ruling that overturned everything.

2014–2018
Construction phase under President Varela

First Quantum invests over $10 billion in capex over 5 years. 56 km of internal roads built, plant assembled, port constructed at Punta Rincón, 300-MW power plant erected, accommodation for ~7,000 workers. Construction workforce peaks at 16,000.

Dec 2017
Supreme Court rules Law 9 of 1997 unconstitutional

The original 1997 contract is found to violate constitutional requirements. The decision sits unresolved for years — production proceeds under a legal cloud while a new contract is negotiated.

Sep 2019
Commercial production begins

First copper concentrate ships. Production ramps from initial 150,000 t (2019) toward full capacity 300-350,000 t by 2021. KORES still holds 10%.

2020–2022
Peak operation · $3.5B annual revenue

Cobre Panamá produces 300,000+ tonnes Cu/yr at full ramp. Becomes 40% of FQM's global revenue. Panama's exports become 75% copper. The mine contributes 3.5% of GDP, 7,000 direct jobs, 40,000 indirect.

2021–2023
Negotiations on a new contract under Cortizo

President Laurentino "Nito" Cortizo's administration pushes a renegotiation. Trade Minister Federico Alfaro Boyd leads. Framework agreement reached in 2021: $375M/year minimum payment (vs. ~$30M under old contract).

Oct 20, 2023
National Assembly ratifies Law 406

The new 20-year concession (renewable for another 20). Royalties of $375M/year minimum, environmental commitments, hiring quotas. Signed by President Cortizo October 21. Protests erupt the same week.

Oct 23 – Nov 28, 2023
The protest wave · largest in Panama's modern history

SUNTRACS-led construction unions block the Inter-American Highway for 5+ weeks. Economy loses ~$1.7B. Demands center on the contract itself. Protests led by Saúl Méndez (SUNTRACS), backed by FRENADESO, environmental NGOs, and student movements. Public figures like Rubén Blades amplify opposition.

Nov 28, 2023
Supreme Court rules 9–0 · Law 406 unconstitutional

Justices Olmedo Arrocha, María Eugenia López, Maribel Cornejo and six colleagues issue unanimous ruling citing 25+ violations. Key findings: operating without legally binding comprehensive environmental program; mine location within Mesoamerican Biological Corridor threatens right to life and health; ESIA from 2011 was outdated.

Nov 29, 2023
Cortizo orders "orderly and safe closure"

Production halts. Mine enters care and maintenance. ~3,500 of 7,000 direct workers laid off over following months. 120,000+ tonnes of finished concentrate stranded at the site, blocked from export.

Nov 14 + 29, 2023
FQM files two arbitration tracks

(1) Notice of intent under Canada-Panama Free Trade Agreement. (2) MPSA initiates ICC arbitration in Miami under the 2023 concession agreement. Total claims to be valued at over $20 billion.

07 · THE 2023 CRISIS · DAY BY DAY

Five weeks that shut a country.

From the contract's signing to the Supreme Court ruling, the most consequential protest movement in Panama since 1989.

Oct 20
Asamblea Nacional ratifies Law 406

National Assembly approves the new 20-year concession contract with FQM. Vote contested by minority bloc. President Cortizo signs into law October 21.

Oct 23
SUNTRACS and FRENADESO call nationwide protest

Saúl Méndez (SUNTRACS Secretary General) declares "indefinite strike." Highway blockades begin on the Inter-American Highway near Aguadulce, then Santiago de Veraguas, then nationally.

Oct 25–28
Inter-American Highway blocked end-to-end

Truck deliveries cease across the country. Food and fuel shortages emerge in interior provinces. Schools close. The Penonomé–David corridor effectively cut.

Oct 28
Cortizo proposes referendum

President offers a national referendum to let the public decide. Asamblea Nacional drafts Law 1100 to authorize the vote. Opposition rejects the proposal as a delay tactic.

Nov 1–3
Death of a protester in Chame · violence escalates

Kenneth Darlington, a 77-year-old US citizen, shoots and kills 2 protesters during a confrontation at a roadblock in Chame. National outrage. Darlington arrested and charged. Protest movement intensifies.

Nov 3
Constitutional moratorium law passed

Asamblea passes Law 407 establishing a moratorium on new metallic mining concessions in Panama. Does not affect Law 406 retroactively. Seen as a partial concession by Cortizo to defuse protests.

Nov 7–20
Economy seizes up

Panamanian Chamber of Commerce estimates $80M/day in economic losses. Tourism collapses. Construction halts. Schools remain closed. Mulino, then opposition presidential candidate, calls for restraint but does not condemn the protests.

Nov 22
Supreme Court begins constitutional review

Multiple constitutional challenges to Law 406 consolidate. Court convenes plenary session to hear arguments. Oral arguments held with both sides.

Nov 28
The 9–0 ruling

Supreme Court unanimously declares Law 406 unconstitutional. President of the Court María Eugenia López reads the decision. 25+ provisions found in violation of the constitution.

Nov 29
Cortizo orders closure

Announces "orderly and safe closure of the mining operation." Production halts. Care and maintenance phase begins. Roadblocks gradually lifted over the following week.

Dec 14
FQM files ICC arbitration

Minera Panamá initiates arbitration proceedings before the International Chamber of Commerce in Miami. Initial demand: full investment recovery + damages. The figure later escalates to $20 billion.

THE PROTEST MOVEMENT IN NUMBERS

~500,000 people in the streets at peak (Nov 5 March of National Dignity). 5+ weeks of highway blockades. ~$1.7 billion in lost economic activity. 3 deaths directly attributed to protest incidents. Approval rating of Cortizo administration dropped from 38% to under 20% during the crisis.

THE OPERATIVE LANGUAGE

The Court ruled that Law 406 was "inconstitutional in its entirety." This is the strongest possible holding — the entire contract is void, not severable. No legal framework currently exists for the mine to operate. Any restart requires a NEW law passing the Asamblea Nacional under stricter constitutional standards.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR ANY RESTART

Any new operating framework must: (a) include legally binding proactive environmental program, (b) be supported by a new ESIA, (c) respect the Mesoamerican Biological Corridor designation, (d) involve formal indigenous consultation, (e) submit to normal fiscal review, (f) have term + scope within constitutional limits, (g) pass the Asamblea Nacional. Mulino has no path to restart by decree.

13 · THE OPPOSITION · WHO STOPPED THE MINE

A coalition that shut a country.

The protest movement was not one organization. It was construction unions, environmental NGOs, students, indigenous communities, and cultural figures — joined around one demand.

The March 2025 decision was a strategic concession in service of value.

SCOPE OF THE STOCKPILE PROCESSING
WHAT IT IS
Processing of already-extracted ore

Material was mined before November 2023 and stockpiled. No new extraction, drilling, or blasting. The plant grinds + concentrates what's already there. Stockpile contains an estimated 70,000 tonnes of recoverable copper across 38 million tonnes of variable-grade ore.

WHAT IT'S NOT
This is not a mine restart

Both FQM and the government explicitly emphasize: processing the stockpile is environmental mitigation + economic salvage, not legal authorization to resume mining. Restart still requires a new constitutional contract via the Asamblea Nacional.

WHY IT WAS APPROVED · THE OFFICIAL JUSTIFICATION

The Government framed approval as environmental risk mitigation: extended outdoor stockpile storage creates acid rock drainage risk to local watersheds. Processing converts the material into concentrate (manageable) and tailings (stored in the engineered tailings facility). The fiscal benefit is incidental, technically. In practice, the move generates ~$700M+ in copper revenue at current prices — meaningful to both parties.

EARLY FINDINGS (preliminary reports released)
  • · Acid rock drainage is the dominant environmental concern. Audit, ~85% complete in mid-March 2026, highlighted this risk as central — and validated stockpile processing as a viable mitigation.
  • · FQM disputes that the mine uses mercury, sulphuric acid (in non-internal context), or cyanide — categorized by company as "scare words" used by NGOs.
  • · Tailings facility integrity confirmed as stable during care + maintenance.
  • · Watershed impact studies inconclusive on long-term sediment changes — additional sampling required.

Mulino had publicly promised a decision by June 2026. The audit was the analytical basis for that decision. With the audit pushed to indefinite future, the June timeline is no longer credible. Two interpretations: (a) genuine technical complexity, (b) political tactical delay to avoid making an unpopular decision during a sensitive moment. Both are likely true.

18 · THE ENVIRONMENTAL DIMENSION

The biological corridor and the watershed.

The mine sits inside the Mesoamerican Biological Corridor — one of the most biodiverse regions on Earth. The 2023 ruling cited this directly. The environmental case is not academic.

CORE ENVIRONMENTAL CONCERNS
CONCERN 01 · ACTIVE
Acid rock drainage

Open stockpiles of sulfide-bearing ore generate acidic leachate when exposed to rain. Without processing, this can leach into local watersheds. Was the official environmental justification for April 2026 stockpile-processing approval.

CONCERN 02 · ACTIVE
Tailings integrity

The tailings storage facility (TSF) is a massive engineered structure holding ~500 million tonnes of mine waste. Long-term seismic + hydrological stability is a permanent risk. SGS audit confirmed current integrity during care + maintenance.

CONCERN 03 · CONSTITUTIONAL
Mesoamerican Biological Corridor

The mine sits within the legally protected corridor. The Supreme Court's 2023 ruling cited this directly as part of the constitutional violation. Any restart must address this designation explicitly.

CONCERN 04 · CONTESTED
Mercury / cyanide / sulfuric acid

NGO claims that the mine uses these compounds. FQM explicitly denies all three — categorizes the claims as "scare words." The truth: small amounts of sulfuric acid are used in copper concentrate processing globally; the mine maintains it does not depend on imported mercury or cyanide.

CONTESTED HEALTH SIGNAL
KIDNEY DISEASE NEAR THE SITE

The Guardian reported an increase in chronic kidney disease in communities near the mine since 2014, when site development began. The relationship is suggestive but not causally established. Possible causes include: heat stress, water quality changes, dust exposure, ergonomic factors. Independent epidemiological study has not been completed. SGS audit did not establish causation.

WATER + AIR
WATER MANAGEMENT
Closed-loop on paper

FQM maintains the mine uses a closed-loop water recycling system and does not draw from the Panama Canal watershed (a common but inaccurate claim — Cobre Panamá is 60+ km from the canal). Local streams (Río Caimito, Río San Juan) drainage area overlaps the concession.

AIR + DUST
Dust + power-plant emissions

Open-pit operations generate dust. On-site coal power plant emits SO₂ + particulates. FQM has emission controls in place but local residents report respiratory complaints. Continuous monitoring is contractually required.

THE REAL ENVIRONMENTAL FRAME FOR ANY RESTART

The legal requirements set by the 2023 ruling are concrete: (1) updated ESIA from 2026 (the 2011 one is invalid), (2) a legally binding proactive environmental program (not reactive mitigation), (3) explicit acknowledgment of the Mesoamerican Biological Corridor designation, (4) consultation with Ngäbe-Buglé authorities, (5) financial assurance bond commensurate with closure costs, (6) third-party environmental monitoring with public reporting.

20 · WHAT COMES NEXT · 2026–2028 SCENARIOS

Four paths through the year.

Mulino has a decision to make. The audit will eventually arrive. The arbitration tracks can be reactivated. These are the realistic scenarios.

SCENARIO A · PHASED RESTART (LIKELIEST)
PROBABILITY: ~55%

Path: Audit releases late 2026 → Cabinet decision Q4 2026 → Asamblea draft new mining law early 2027 → vote mid-2027 → restart H2 2027. FQM concedes on state ownership of resources, environmental bonding, increased royalty. Stockpile processing continues throughout. Production resumes 2028. Outcome: 200-300K t Cu/yr at full ramp by 2029, but under significantly stricter terms.

SCENARIO B · INDEFINITE LIMBO
PROBABILITY: ~25%

Path: Audit reveals environmental issues that make restart politically impossible. Stockpile processing concludes early 2027 with ~70K t Cu sold. Mulino punts decision to next administration (2029). Mine stays in indefinite care + maintenance ($15-20M/year). Outcome: Slow value erosion. KORES arbitration proceeds. Workforce shrinks. The asset becomes a stranded $10B investment.

SCENARIO C · ARBITRATION REACTIVATION
PROBABILITY: ~12%

Path: Negotiations collapse. FQM reactivates Canada-Panama FTA arbitration (suspended, not discontinued). KORES claim proceeds. Decisions arrive 2028-2030 — potentially $5-20B award to FQM. Outcome: Win on damages, lose the asset. Panama becomes much harder for future mining capital. Long-term reputation damage to both sides.

SCENARIO D · STRATEGIC PARTNER ENTERS
PROBABILITY: ~8%

Path: A new state-anchored investor (Saudi Manara, Japanese consortium, or even a Panamanian state co-investment vehicle) takes meaningful equity in MPSA. Provides political cover + capital + diplomatic shield. Restart 2028 under tripartite governance. Outcome: Less revenue per tonne for FQM, but lower political risk going forward. Could reshape the Latin American mining template.

WHAT WATCHERS ARE TRACKING

(1) The SGS final report (delayed; expected H2 2026). (2) Mulino's mid-year address — will it commit to a decision? (3) FQM Q2 2026 earnings — restart timeline guidance. (4) Asamblea Nacional calendar — any draft mining law? (5) KORES arbitration filings. (6) Public sentiment polls in Donoso vs. Panama City — divergence is widening. (7) Copper price (currently ~$10K/t) — every $1K/t move shifts the negotiation math by ~$300M annually.

IMPLICATIONS FOR VETA + LA COMPAÑÍA

Three structural lessons for any new Panama mining initiative: (1) Asamblea ratification is non-negotiable — bilateral concession without legislative cover is fragile. (2) Indigenous consultation is now constitutional — Ngäbe-Buglé and other Comarca-adjacent projects need congresos generales aligned from day one. (3) Streaming + JV structures are politically more viable than equity concessions — a Panamanian state entity retaining mineral title and granting metals streams to operators is what Mulino's team is exploring. The era of generous bilateral concessions is over.
