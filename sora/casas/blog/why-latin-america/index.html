The thesis

Why Latin America

The case for moving, buying, or building south — and why Panama, specifically, is where most of that case actually holds up.

Abraham Eid·Founder, Sora Casas·Boquete, Chiriquí·June 2026·9 min read

Most people who end up in Panama did not set out to move to Latin America. They set out to escape a number — a property-tax bill, a January heating bill, a retirement projection that quietly stopped adding up somewhere around 2021. Latin America is where that math starts working again. Panama is where, for a foreign buyer, it works without the trade-offs that usually come attached.

I build homes here, in the Chiriquí highlands and on the Azuero coast, for people doing exactly this. That gives me a biased seat. It also means the same five questions come up on almost every first call, and the honest answers to those five questions are the entire argument. So here they are, in order.

Your money buys more — and it is still your money

The first thing people picture is a cheaper grocery bill. That part is real, but it is the least interesting part. Three structural facts matter more.

Panama runs on the US dollar. It has since 1904. The balboa is pegged one-to-one and mostly exists as coins, so you price your house, pay your contractor, and hold your savings in the same currency you already use. There is no conversion spread eating your budget and no overnight devaluation to plan around — the failure mode that has burned foreign buyers in Argentina, Mexico, and Brazil for decades.

Panama also taxes income on a territorial basis. It taxes money earned inside Panama. A pension, dividends from a brokerage account at home, a salary a company abroad pays you for remote work — Panama does not treat any of that as taxable. Your home country may still want its share; US citizens owe US tax on worldwide income wherever they live. But Panama does not stack a second bill on top of the first.

And if you arrive on a pension, the discounts are written into law rather than offered as a courtesy. A partial list: 25% off restaurant meals, 25% off airline tickets, roughly 20% off doctors' bills, 50% off movies and entertainment, and 25% off your electric and phone bills. None of that is a loyalty program someone can cancel. It is statute.

The number people remember

A 200 m² (≈2,150 sq ft) turnkey home in our highland and coast markets runs roughly $280,000–$345,000 to build, before land. That figure does more work in Boquete than three times the figure does in most of California or the Northeast.

You don't have to disappear to do it

This is the question that quietly decides everything, and almost no one asks it first. Portugal is a seven-to-eleven-hour flight and five-to-eight time zones from US family. Thailand is a different day. When a grandchild is born or a parent falls ill, that distance turns from a line on a map into a real cost you pay several times a year.

Panama City is about a three-hour flight from Miami and under six from New York or Toronto. The country sits in the US Eastern time zone and does not change its clocks, so for half the year it shares the hour with the East Coast and is one off the Midwest. You can take a 7 a.m. call with the office, drive to the beach by lunch, and be back for a kid's bedtime story over video without doing arithmetic. Proximity is the feature that makes the rest of the list usable instead of theoretical.

Residency is written down, not granted by mood

In a lot of the region, the legal path to staying is vague enough that you end up depending on a fixer and hoping. Panama's routes are specific, and which one fits you is a question of income versus capital, not luck.

  • Pensionado. If you have a guaranteed lifetime pension of at least $1,000 a month, this is usually the cleanest path, and it is the one that carries the legislated discounts above.
  • Friendly Nations Visa. For citizens of a long list of countries, $200,000 in titled real estate qualifies you for a residency route that leads to permanent status.
  • Qualified Investor Visa. Starts at $300,000 in real estate — a floor that has already been raised once and is set to climb again, so the figure you read today is most likely the lowest it will be.

The point is not which one is best. It is that you can read the requirement, meet it on paper, and know where you stand. That is rarer than it sounds.

You choose your climate by elevation, not by season

Panama is nine degrees north of the equator, so there is no winter to wait out. What there is instead is altitude. Drive up and the temperature drops; drive down and it climbs. You pick the climate you want and then buy the elevation that produces it.

Boquete sits around 1,200 meters in the Chiriquí highlands, which gives it the daytimes-in-the-low-70s, cool-nights weather expats describe as eternal spring — the reason it has the largest established foreign community outside Panama City. An hour higher, Volcán and Cerro Punta are cooler and quieter and cost less per hectare. Drop to the Azuero coast around Pedasí and you trade the sweaters for a dry, hot, surf-and-sand pattern with the lightest rainfall in the country. These are ninety minutes apart. Most buyers visit certain they want the beach and leave having bought in the mountains, or the reverse — which is the entire reason we tell people to come stand on the land before they decide anything.

The part nobody puts in the brochure

If a guide to moving here reads like pure upside, it was written to sell you something. The honest version has three warnings.

First, and most important: titled land is not the only kind of land for sale here. A large share of property changes hands as derecho posesorio — rights of possession. It looks like ownership and is often priced like it, but it is not registered title, it cannot be insured, it is hard to finance, and it can be miserable to resell. The single most important sentence in any Panama purchase is the lawyer confirming, in the Public Registry, that what you are buying is título de propiedad before a dollar moves. We will not option a lot for a client until that check clears.

Second, the bureaucracy is real and runs on relationships and patience, not on a web portal. Permits, utility connections, and registry steps take longer than a North American expects, and the fastest way through is people who already know the clerk, the inspector, and the surveyor.

Third, you need those people. The buyers who struggle are the ones who tried to do it remotely from a laptop, trusting whoever answered the email. The ones who do well had someone on the ground they could trust before they wired anything.

Why Panama, and not the rest of the region

Everything above — dollars, low taxes, written residency, climate by elevation — is the regional pitch. Plenty of countries have one or two of those. Panama is the place that holds all of them at once and then adds the things that let you sleep: a dollarized, banked economy; an unusually stable legal and political track record for the region; and a country built as a hub, with the busiest air connections in Central America and a canal economy that keeps the lights on regardless of who wins an election. You are not betting on a frontier. You are buying into the part of Latin America that has already done the boring work of being predictable.

That is the case, with the caveats left in. If it sounds like the trade you are looking for, the next decision is not really "Panama, yes or no." It is highland or coast, buy or build, and which lot is actually titled. That part we can help with — it is the only thing we do.
